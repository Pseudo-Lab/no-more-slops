Teams building large language model (LLM) and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) applications rely on small evaluation sets, often called golden datasets, for regression testing, model selection, and calibration of automatic judges. Yet there is no agreed definition of what makes a dataset "golden," and widely used public benchmarks have been found to contain label errors at rates of a few percent overall and, in some subsets, in more than half of the analyzed items. This mini review synthesizes literature, mainly from 2015 to 2026, on dataset construction, quality validation, and documentation. Construction methods fall into expert authoring, curation of real user logs, LLM-based synthesis, and adversarial collection, and none of them secures correctness, realism, and low cost at once; RAG sets additionally require checks on the completeness of relevance judgments and attribution labels. Validation can be organized into five levels (construct validity, label reliability, error auditing, statistical adequacy, and contamination control), and the literature repeatedly reports sets that passed one level yet failed another. We propose that "golden" be defined not as an intrinsic property but as a state in which evidence that seven conditions are met is documented for a stated purpose and version, and we present a two-tier design in which an audited golden core calibrates a larger auxiliary set. We close by discussing three controversies (scale versus quality, a single answer versus perspectivism, and human versus LLM labels) and open research problems.
